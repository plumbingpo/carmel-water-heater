No hot water from a gas heater usually means one thing: the burner is not firing, and something told it not to. Gas heaters are built around safety. The control valve will not open unless a thermocouple or flame sensor proves there is a flame, and many models lock out if the flame arrestor screen at the bottom gets clogged or the heater senses spilled exhaust. So the real question is which safety did its job, and why. Warning Signs to Watch For

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • A pilot light that will not stay lit
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • No hot water at all, with the burner never firing
  • Showers that go cold sooner than they did a year ago
  • Any smell of gas around the appliance, which calls for leaving and phoning the gas utility
  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • A tank that pops and bangs while it heats, a classic sign of sediment

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Two things make gas water heater work a poor DIY project: the parts involve fuel and combustion, and the symptoms overlap. A pilot outage might come from a worn thermocouple, a partly blocked pilot tube, lint on the flame arrestor screen, a downdraft, or an internal valve failure. A wrong guess there just wastes money. Guess wrong on a gas fitting or vent connection and the stakes are much higher, since a small leak or a spillage problem can put gas or carbon monoxide into the living space. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically call for a permit.

Our technicians approach gas heaters with instruments rather than assumptions. They measure thermocouple millivolts, read gas pressure at the valve, test draft at the hood, check for CO around the appliance, and leak-test every joint they touch. That process fixes the actual fault the first time and leaves your home safer than before. We also give you an honest assessment of the heater itself. A tank past about ten or twelve years with a failing valve or a leak is usually a replacement, and you will hear that from us before you spend money on a repair.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Install carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home and test them monthly
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Keep the thermostat around 120 degrees to save energy and reduce scalding risk
  • Flush the tank on a regular schedule so sediment never bakes onto the bottom
  • Never store flammable liquids near a gas water heater
  • Check the anode rod every three to five years, since it protects the tank from corrosion
